# Impulse and Momentum for Particles
To solve a system with the least amount of effort
1. Try solving with **[conservation of energy](Conservation%20of%20Energy.md)**.
2. If **1.** is not possible[^impos1], try solving with **impulse and momentum**.
3. If **2.** is not possible[^impos2], try solving by **[integrating the equations of motion](Particle%20Mechanics.md)**.

Following this list ensures that you spend the least amount of time possible on solutions.

### Interpretation
***Momentum***, $\vec{\textbf{p}}$, is a quantity of motion that all moving particles have. It is the product of the mass and the velocity of that particle. This combination describes the resistance the particle has to changes in motion (with the mass), and the motion it currently has (with the velocity). <br>If no external factors act on this particle it's momentum will be conserved. <br>***Impulse***, $\vec{\textbf{J}}$, on the other hand, describes a change in momentum. Imagine a baseball being struck by a bat. Before being struck the baseball has a certain momentum, moving in a certain direction. It's direction and it's speed are changed by the impact of the bat. This change in momentum is caused by the impulse that the bat exerts onto the baseball. Note that impulse can only be generated by external [forces](Force), not any internal ones. <br> Impulse is a force over a certain amount of time. During short impacts it is very hard to determine the magnitude of the force on the bodies and it can be especially tough to determine how the force changes over time during the impact. This information, however, is necessary if you want to use integration from the equations of motion to understand the motion of the system. For an impulse the *average force* during the duration of the impact is used, or it can be inferred by measuring the momentum before and after, which is very easy to do with little equipment. <br><br>When a body is struck quick and hard the body may deform significantly. This causes the release of chemical energy within the body which we are hard to compute with conservation of energy. As such the equations for impulse and momentum are used to replace conservation of energy in these scenarios.

>[!note]
> While using the momentum equations are very helpful to understand situations with very short impacts, they **apply regardless of the duration of the external force**. 
> Because $\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{F}} t$, impulse can be used to compute the time it takes for a box to be stopped from moving as a result of friction. The immediate availability of $t$ in the equation for impulse means that using this equation can save a significant amount of time as opposed to finding it by integrating the equations of motion.

### Mathematical Formulation
The ***momentum**** of a particle is defined as 
$$
\vec{\textbf{p}} = m \vec{\textbf{v}}
$$
This implies that the higher the mass and the bigger the velocity of a particle are, the harder it becomes to make this particle change direction, and the bigger the necessary impulse is. <br>The ***impulse**** exerted on a particle is defined as the change in momentum.
$$
\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{p}}_2 - \vec{\textbf{p}}_1
$$
This impulse is caused by an (average) [force](Force) that acts for a certain duration ($\Delta t$) on the particle. We can thus alternatively write[^integ]
$$
\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{F}} \Delta t \qquad \qquad \vec{\textbf{J}} = \int_{t_1} ^{t_2} \vec{\textbf{F}}(t)dt
$$
To increase the impulse we can thus either increase the force, or we can make the force act on the particle for longer. 
##### Analyzing Systems
Because of the way we defined the impulse, every system with particles is always subject to the equation
$$
\vec{\textbf{p}}_1 + \vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{p}}_2
$$
Where $\vec{\textbf{p}}_1$ is the momentum just before the impulse acts on the particle, and $\vec{\textbf{p}}_2$ is the momentum directly after the particle escapes the impulse. <br>We see that the momentum after the impact, $\vec{\textbf{p}}_2$, is described by the moment the particle had before any [force](Force) acted on it combined with the motion that the force introduces. <br><br>We may want to analyze a system where two bodies collide and analyze the motion of both bodies after the collision simultaneously. An example would be shooting a block of wood with a bullet, and wanting to know with what speed the wood moves after impact. In this case we consider the impact of the bullet hitting the block of wood internal to the system, because we are analyzing both bodies at the same time. Because the force the bullet exerts on the block is thus an internal one, though seemingly contradictory, the impulse in this case is 0. <br>For this system the initial impulse $\vec{\textbf{p}}_1$ is made up of the sum of the momentum of both bodies, and the final impulse $\vec{\textbf{p}}_2$ is made up of the combined mass of the two bodies and the exiting speed we want to find.
$$
\vec{\textbf{p}}_1 + \vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{p}}_2 \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad [m_\text{bullet} \vec{\textbf{v}}_\text{bullet} + m_\text{block} \vec{\textbf{v}}_\text{block}]_1 + 0 = (m_\text{bullet} + m_\text{block}) \vec{\textbf{v}}_2
$$



### Inventory
> [!abstract] Inventory of the Formulas
> - For the **momentum**
> $$
> \vec{\textbf{p}} = m \vec{\textbf{v}}
> $$
> - For the **impulse**
> $$
> \vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{F}} \Delta t
> $$
> - For **particle systems**
> $$
> \vec{\textbf{p}}_1 + \vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{p}}_2
> $$

[^impos1]: Solving with conservation of energy may be impossible because there are too many unknown properties in the resulting equations, or because an impact is present that deforms the body in such a way that a non-negligible amount of chemical energy is lost in the material. We cannot easily compute the amount of chemical energy expended during an impact and for this reason we say that conservation of energy does not hold in these situations.
[^impos2]: Solving with impulse and momentum may be impossible because there are too many unknown properties in the resulting equations.
[^integ]: This form is a simplification of the differential equation $d\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{F}}(t) dt$ and the resulting integral $\vec{\textbf{J}} = \int \vec{\textbf{F}}(t) dt$. Because we use an average for the force and not a time dependent variable, we can bring it out of the integral and integrate to obtain $\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{F}} \Delta t$.
