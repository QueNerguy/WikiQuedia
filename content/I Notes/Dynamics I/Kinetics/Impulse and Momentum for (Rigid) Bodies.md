# Impulse and Momentum for (Rigid) Bodies
To solve a system with the least amount of effort
1. Try solving with **[conservation of energy](Conservation%20of%20Energy.md)**.
2. If **1.** is not possible[^impos1], try solving with **impulse and momentum**.
3. If **2.** is not possible[^impos2], try solving by **[integrating the equations of motion](Rigid%20Body%20Mechanics.md)**.

Following this list ensures that you spend the least amount of time possible on solutions.


### Interpretation
The equations for *impulse* and *momentum* for particles hold also for rigid bodies in the case of translating motions. This note will describe the equations for impulse and momentum for rotations. <br>***Angular Momentum***, $\vec{\textbf{L}}$,[^altnote] is a quantity of rotational motion that all moving bodies have. It is the product of the [inertia](Inertia%20-%20Mass%20Moment.md) and the [angular velocity](Angular%20Velocity) of that body. This combination describes the resistance the body has to changes in motion (with the [mass moment of inertia](Inertia%20-%20Mass%20Moment.md)), and the motion it currently has (with the angular velocity). <br>If no external factors act on this body it's angular momentum will be conserved.<br>***Angular impulse***, $\vec{\textbf{J}}$, on the other hand, describes a change in momentum. Imagine a baseball being struck off-center by a bat. Before being struck the baseball has a certain momentum, moving in a certain direction with almost no spin. It's direction, it's speed and it's rotation are changed significantly by the impact of the bat. This change in momentum is caused by the impulse that the bat exerts onto the baseball. Note that impulse can only be generated by external [forces](Force) or [moments](Moment), not any internal ones. <br>Impulse is a moment[^whattypeofmoment] over a certain amount of time. During short impacts it is very hard to determine the magnitude of these moments on the bodies and it can be especially tough to determine how the moment changes over time during the impact. This information, however, is necessary if you want to use integration from the equations of motion to understand the motion of the system. For an impulse the *average moment* during the duration of the impact is used, or it can be inferred by measuring the momentum before and after, which is very easy to do with little equipment. <br><br>When a body is struck quick and hard the body may deform significantly. This causes the release of chemical energy within the body which we are hard to compute with conservation of energy. As such the equations for impulse and momentum are used to replace conservation of energy in these scenarios.

>[!note]
> While using the momentum equations are very helpful to understand situations with very short impacts, they **apply regardless of the duration of the external force**. 
> Because $\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{F}} t$, impulse can be used to compute the time it takes for a box to be stopped from moving as a result of friction. The immediate availability of $t$ in the equation for impulse means that using this equation can save a significant amount of time as opposed to finding it by integrating the equations of motion.




### Mathematical Formulation
The ***angular momentum*** of a particle around some point $O$ is defined as 
$$
\vec{\textbf{L}}_O = I_O \vec{\pmb{\omega}} + \vec{\textbf{r}}_{O/G} \times \vec{\textbf{p}}
$$
Or in scalar form we have
$$
L = I_O \omega + rp
$$
Where $p$ ($= mv$) denotes the linear momentum and $r$ denotes the distance between our point $O$ and the [ICR](Rigid%20Body%20Motion.md#Instant%20Center%20of%20Rotation), $G$. Keep in mind that $r$ needs to make a right angle with the direction of motion, the direction of $v$. Also note that for the [inertia](Inertia%20-%20Mass%20Moment.md) $I_O$ is used, not $I$. To find $I_O$ we compute the inertia of the body around it's center of rotation, and then transfer to our chosen point[^noteref]. To explain this formulation, notice that this equation is made up of two parts:
1. $I_O \vec{\pmb{\omega}}$
2. $\vec{\textbf{r}}_{O/G} \times \vec{\textbf{p}}$, alternatively written as  $\vec{\textbf{r}}_{O/G} \times m \vec{\textbf{v}}$

Part **1.** is the momentum resulting from the *inherent spin of the object*. Part **2.** is the momentum resulting from the rotation as observed from our point O. This term applies even when the object itself, as viewed from it's ICR, doesn't rotate. When an motorcycle rides past us, we see a different part of it's body before it has passed us than we see after the motorcycle has passed us. The fact that we see a different part of the bike in both these instances implies that, as observed from our standpoint, the motorcycle rotated, on top of moving past us (translating) even though the rider does not observe this rotation. Even though this rotation is *imaginary*, it still has some momentum. <br><br>Note that when an object makes contact with another body it may rotate purely around this contact point *so long as there is contact*. After the bodies don't connect anymore it will start to rotate around it's natural center of rotation[^nattie]. This means that from the moment the bodies make contact to the moment the contact stops the second term disappears, because the distance between the contact point $O$ and the ICR $G$ is 0, our contact point has *become* the ICR. <br>In the same vein, the **angular momentum around the ICR** $G$ also has no second term, because $\vec{\textbf{r}}_{G/G} = 0$.
$$
\vec{\textbf{L}}_G = I \vec{\pmb{\omega}}
$$
<br>The ***angular impulse*** exerted on a body is defined as the change in momentum.
$$
\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{L}}_2 - \vec{\textbf{L}}_1
$$
This impulse is caused by an (average) [moment](Moment)[^whattypeofmoment] that acts for a certain duration ($\Delta t$) on the particle. We can thus alternatively write[^integ]
$$
\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{M}} \Delta t \qquad \qquad \vec{\textbf{J}} = \int_{t_1} ^{t_2} \vec{\textbf{M}}(t)dt
$$
To increase the impulse we can thus either increase the moment, or we can make the moment act on the particle for longer. 

##### Analyzing Systems
Because of the way we defined the angular impulse, every system with particles is always subject to the equation
$$
\vec{\textbf{L}}_1 + \vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{L}}_2
$$
Where $\vec{\textbf{L}}_1$ is the momentum just before the impulse acts on the particle, and $\vec{\textbf{L}}_2$ is the momentum directly after the particle escapes the impulse. <br>We see that the momentum after the impact, $\vec{\textbf{L}}_2$, is described by the moment the particle had before any [force](Force) or [moment](Moment) acted on it combined with the motion that the force and moment introduce. 

### Inventory
> [!abstract] Inventory of the Formulas
> - For the **angular momentum about some point** $O$
> $$
> \vec{\textbf{L}}_O = I_O \vec{\pmb{\omega}} + \vec{\textbf{r}}_{O/G} \times \vec{\textbf{p}}
> $$
> - For the **angular momentum about the [ICR](Rigid%20Body%20Motion.md#Instant%20Center%20of%20Rotation)** $G$
> $$
> \vec{\textbf{L}}_G = I \vec{\pmb{\omega}}
> $$
> - For the **angular impulse**
> $$
> \vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{M}} \Delta t
> $$
> - For **rigid body systems**
> $$
> \vec{\textbf{L}}_1 + \vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{L}}_2
> $$

[^impos1]: Solving with conservation of energy may be impossible because there are too many unknown properties in the resulting equations, or because an impact is present that deforms the body in such a way that a non-negligible amount of chemical energy is lost in the material. We cannot easily compute the amount of chemical energy expended during an impact and for this reason we say that conservation of energy does not hold in these situations.
[^impos2]: Solving with impulse and momentum may be impossible because there are too many unknown properties in the resulting equations.
[^whattypeofmoment]: These moments can be caused by the [torque](Torque) of for example a motor, or by the imaginary [moment](Moment) caused by applying a force that's not going through the center of rotation (recall that $\vec{\textbf{M}} = \vec{\textbf{r}} \times \vec{\textbf{F}}$). An example of this would be grazing a baseball, this would cause it to rotate significantly, meaning that a good angular impulse was applied to the body in order to cause this rotation.
[^altnote]: In textbooks you may also see $\vec{\textbf{H}}$ used to refer to angular momentum instead of $\vec{\textbf{L}}$.
[^noteref]: This transfer of our axes is described in detail [here](Inertia%20-%20Mass%20Moment.md#Transfer%20of%20Axes).
[^nattie]: In the case of a rotation with no external forces this rotation will be about it's center of gravity.
[^integ]: This form is a simplification of the differential equation $d\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{M}}(t) dt$ and the resulting integral $\vec{\textbf{J}} = \int \vec{\textbf{M}}(t) dt$. Because we use an average for the moment and not a time dependent variable, we can bring it out of the integral and integrate to obtain $\vec{\textbf{J}} = \vec{\textbf{M}} \Delta t$.
